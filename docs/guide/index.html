Everything the app does, and where to find it.

One page per part of a run: getting ready, running, and what the app does with it afterwards. Written to be looked things up in, not read end to end.

Newest thing

Your whole route library has been brought into line, in one go. The app changed how it tidies a course, but only for courses arriving after that — everything already in your library was still stored the old way, so the app could be handed one of your own route files and not recognise it. Updating the app redraws every route once, the new way. A distance can come out slightly shorter (about 47 m on one real 4.20 km course — the corners the tidying cuts), a course you happened to keep twice becomes one row under the name you have had longest, and no climb is worked out again, because a route keeps no heights to work one out from — the only climb that moves is a merged row that had none taking the one its copy had. Names, dates and your runs are untouched. See What this update did to your library in routes.

One run gives you one route, however you save it. If you saved a run as a route and shared that same run as a .gpx and imported the file back, you used to end up with two routes, both named after the same evening, with no way to tell them apart. The two doors tidied the course differently — one kept every point the run recorded, the other trimmed it to its shape — so the app read them as two different courses. Both doors now tidy it the same way, so the second one is recognised as the route you already have and its numbers are refreshed from the file, instead of a new row appearing. Files from other apps are unchanged: Strava and Komoot trim by their own rules, so a course brought back from one of them is still a route of its own. See Keeping a run as a route and Importing the same course again in routes.

The coach can finally see how long you have been training. A stage that asks for “four weeks of consistent Zone 2 training” was being judged on your last three sessions, which reach back about a week — so it could never confirm four, and it told you three weeks in that your block was only just beginning. The app now counts every run you have done in the stage and hands the coach the count, with the most recent twelve weeks broken out one by one. A week means seven days gone by from the first run the count accepted, so four weeks arrive on day 28 — never early, because moving you up a stage is never undone. The count says how much you trained and never how hard: the recent runs still answer the Zone 2 half. See The coach is shown all of your training in this stage in the training plan.

Delete a route mid-run and it stops talking about it — even a sentence already lined up. If the app had just decided to tell you “Off course.” and that sentence was waiting behind another one, deleting the route used to leave it in the queue, and you heard it seconds later about a course the app no longer had. It is now taken back with the route. The only sentence that still finishes is one already being spoken out loud, because nothing in the app ever cuts a sentence off. See Off course in routes.

Your routes are drawings now, not just names. Every course in your library has a small drawing of its shape beside it — the same drawing your runs have in History, in the same amber line. You can pick the park loop out from the canal out-and-back without reading a word. A course too small to have a shape — one whose whole course fits inside sixty metres across — keeps an empty square rather than a dot pretending to be a route. See The library in routes.

Two things the app does when nobody is watching, done better. Every time you open the app it quietly finishes off jobs left over from last time — filling in old numbers, rescuing a run that was never finished. If the very first step of one of those jobs failed, the whole app used to shut itself down before you saw a screen. It now writes the failure down, leaves that job’s work for the next time you open the app, and carries on. What it catches is a job going wrong, which is what a job does. It does not catch the app or the phone being in real trouble underneath — out of room to work in, or something broken in the app itself — and it deliberately does not, because carrying on past one of those is worse than stopping. Separately: stop a run from the notification at the same moment the app is being shut down, and the app’s own rescue can save the run first — correctly, but without knowing you stopped it yourself, so your stage credit was held back until you next fully restarted the app — and, after a Long run, the coach’s words with it. They now arrive straight away, while the app is still open. See If something goes wrong in during the run.

Setting up, once

Four things to get right at the start. After this the app mostly looks after itself.

Your heart-rate strap

The app talks to any standard Bluetooth chest strap — Polar, Garmin, Wahoo. Tap Scan, pick yours, and it is remembered. If you have more than one saved, the one marked Active is preferred.

Before a run the app hunts for the strap on its own. If it cannot find it, it gives up after about two and a half minutes and says so rather than searching forever. If the strap drops out mid-run, the run keeps going and the app keeps trying to get it back.

Where Home → Devices

Your heart rates, and the five zones

Everything the app says about effort is built on two numbers: your maximum heart rate, and your resting one. What the five zones slice is the gap between them — your reserve, the range your heart actually works across.

Resting is optional, and leaving it blank is a real answer rather than a gap: with nothing typed the reserve runs from zero, so the zones come out as plain percentages of your maximum and nothing in your history moves. Type one and every zone edge lifts, because a heart that idles at 55 is not working at all at 55.

ZoneOf your reserveWhat it is
Z1 Endurance50–60%Easy. Conversation pace.
Z2 Moderate60–70%The default target. Where base fitness is built.
Z3 Tempo70–80%Comfortably hard.
Z4 Threshold80–90%Hard. Sustainable for a while, not long.
Z5 Anaerobic90%+Flat out.

The two numbers hold each other apart. They have to leave a usable gap, so each field refuses a number the other cannot live with, and tells you the range it will take. Clearing the resting field asks you first, because it puts your zones back onto your maximum alone.

You don't have to guess the maximum. Until you have ever set one, the Progress screen offers you the highest heart rate it has recorded from you — or, if it has never recorded one, a rough figure from your age. See Progress.

Where Home → Prefs

Permissions

Bluetooth to reach the strap, Location for outdoor runs and for Bluetooth scanning, and Notifications so the run can keep recording with the screen off.

Worth knowing. Taking a permission away while a run is going stops the run. Grant them once and leave them.

Where to keep your backups

Pick a folder once — a Google Drive folder is the sensible choice, because then the backup lives somewhere other than the phone. The app writes a full archive there every month and keeps the last three. See Your data.

Where Home → Prefs → Backup

Before a run

The home screen's job is one decision: what are you doing today, and are you ready to start.

Today's run

The home screen shows the stage of the plan you are in, the session it suggests for today, and the other sessions you could pick instead — usually a Long, an Easy and a Quality one. Each says roughly how long it will take.

If the AI coach has written you a Long run, that is the one shown, with its intervals and its target zone already filled in.

Where Home

Treadmill or Outdoor

Outdoor records GPS as well as heart rate, so you get distance, pace, a map and splits. Treadmill records heart rate only — there is no GPS indoors worth having — and you type the distance in afterwards from the console.

Where Home → the two buttons above START

Following a route

Pick a course out of your routes before you start and the run follows it. Tap Choose a route, pick one from the list, and the card says which one and how far it goes. No route is at the top of the same list, which is how you go back to an ordinary run.

Run it backwards is for doing the course the other way round. It makes no difference to the line on the map — the same ground in the same places — but it does decide which end the map counts down to, so the distance left is measured to the end you are actually running towards.

Outdoor only. There is no ground under a treadmill for a course to be over, so the card is not shown in Treadmill mode. If you pick a route and then switch to Treadmill your choice is kept, in case you switch back — it just does not come along on that run.

Picking a route changes almost nothing else about the run. Same warm-up and cool-down, same intervals, same target zone, same records. It is a line on the map, not a different kind of run. The one thing it adds is the two course cues — “Off course.” and “Back on course.” — which only a run following a route can ever hear. See Off course in routes.

No routes yet? The card says so and tells you where they come from — a GPX under Open Routes, or a run you have already been for.

Where Home → the Route card, under Treadmill / Outdoor

Coach modes

Run/Walk is the beginner mode: the workout is a set of run and walk intervals, and the app calls the changes out loud. Continuous is a straight run in a target zone.

In Run/Walk, a high heart rate is only ever advice — "ease off slightly". The walks are the ones the workout prescribed, and the app will not order you to walk.

Simulate mode

Runs the whole app on invented heart-rate data, so the screens and the coaching can be tried without strapping anything on. It records a real run in your history, so delete it afterwards.

What it can't do. There is no GPS in simulate mode — no distance, no map, no splits.

Where Home → Prefs

During the run

The screen can be off and the phone in your pocket. Recording continues, and the app talks to you.

The three phases

A session the plan gave you runs Warm-up, then the Main workout, then Cool-down. You can skip straight to the cool-down when you have had enough.

Only where there is a workout to run. The phases come from the session, so a run with no plan attached — you tapped Skip today, or there is nothing planned — has none of them. It is open-ended: it starts when you start and it runs until you stop it, with no change ever coming and nothing to skip to. A plan test is the same, deliberately: a 5K flat out with no warm-up or cool-down wrapped round it.

What the screen shows

Top: the phase you are in and the countdown to the next change. Middle: your heart rate and whether it is below, in, or above the target zone. Bottom: elapsed time, distance and pace. Structured workouts also get a timeline strip with a marker where you are, and a chip saying why the last thing happened — a planned change, heart rate too high, heart rate recovered, strap lost.

When the app speaks, and when it doesn't

  • It only speaks while you are meant to be running. The warm-up, every walk interval and the cool-down are silent, because you are deliberately not at your target in them. A run with no plan attached makes its own quiet start instead: the first five minutes, to settle into a pace before being asked for one.
  • It waits before complaining. You must be out of your zone for 30 seconds before it says anything, so a blip is ignored. The edge has a little give in it too, so a heart rate hovering on the line does not set it off.
  • It won't nag. Stay out and it speaks again at one minute, and after that at most once every five minutes, however long you stay out. It never speaks faster than that.
  • It closes the loop. Come back into your zone and it says so once — but only if it had actually told you off, so it never announces a return from somewhere you were never told you had gone.
  • Cardiac drift is recognised. Your heart rate at ten minutes is remembered. After twenty minutes, a heart rate that has crept up only a little above that gets "heart rate drifting up, keep effort steady, or take a short walk break" rather than "ease off". Higher than a little is treated as real overexertion and gets the plain cue.
  • Every kilometre is called out — on an outdoor run, and only while Split announcements is on in Prefs. A treadmill run has no measured ground to cross a kilometre of, so it gets none.
  • "Halfway. Turn around." once, on an outdoor run following a plan, at half the run's total moving time — so an out-and-back gets you home. Switch it off in Prefs.
  • "Off course." and "Back on course." — only on a run following a route, and only about that route. These two sit outside every rule above: they are nothing to do with your heart rate, so the quiet warm-up, the 30-second wait and the five-minute limit have no bearing on them. See Off course in routes.

One queue, so nothing is cut off

Every spoken cue goes into a single ordered queue and they play one at a time, back to back. Navigation goes first, then instructions such as an interval change, then coaching, then information such as a split. Nothing is ever cut off mid-sentence and nothing is silently dropped — and when you press stop, the app stops talking.

Pausing

Pause and resume by hand, or let the app do it: standing still on an outdoor run auto-pauses, and moving again resumes. Paused time is not counted as moving time, and a pause carries no distance.

If the signal drops rather than you stopping, that is different — see Outage in the app's words.

If something goes wrong

Android sometimes takes a recording away from an app in the background. When that happens the app now notices, finishes the run from the seconds already written down, and puts a notification on screen with a sound telling you the run stopped recording. It sends you to your history to look rather than promising the run was saved.

There is one case where it tells you something different. If the recording is taken away in the first instant — between tapping START and the run being written down, which is a fraction of a second — there may be nothing recorded at all. The app now waits for that run to finish being written down, and if it turns out to hold no seconds, it clears it away again rather than leaving an empty run sitting in your history. The notification then says so plainly — The run stopped before it had recorded anything, so there is nothing to save. Start it again. That is deliberately not the same message, because sending you to your history to look for a run that was never written down is a wild goose chase.

There is a third case, and it is the one you are most likely to meet, because it is not a thing going wrong at all — it is you pressing STOP straight away. Perhaps you set off, changed your mind, and stopped within a second or two. Writing a run down takes the app a fraction of a second, and a run stopped inside that fraction has nothing to attach its times to yet, so it holds them and waits. If the app was shut down in that same moment, it used to hold them for ever: the run never appeared in your history, and you were never told, because as far as the app knew you had stopped it yourself and nothing had gone wrong. It now hands those times over on its way out, so the run lands in your history like any other, with the seconds and the heart rate it actually recorded — and you are told nothing, because nothing was taken from you. Two parts of the app can do that handing over, and only one of them may, because doing it twice would write every second down twice. The app used to choose by looking at whether the run’s own clock was still busy, and stood aside when it was — but on a busy phone that clock could be busy with something else entirely, and it had already been shut, so it never picked the job up and the times went. Now whichever of the two is about to do it claims the job first and the other is told it has been taken, so exactly one of them always hands the times over. That handover is still the last thing the app gets to do, though, so if it is shut down faster still, or is left too busy to finish tidying up, the run can be left unfinished after all. That is the 00:00 case, and the note below is the answer to it.

There is a fourth case, and it is the happiest one, because you never lose anything by it. Press STOP from the notification at the very moment the app is being shut down, and the app’s own rescue can reach the run first and save it. It saves it correctly, but it has no way of knowing you stopped it yourself — and a run nobody stopped is not one the training plan is allowed to judge. So the run’s stage credit was set aside as still owed, and nothing asked for it again until you fully closed and reopened the app, which on a phone can be hours. It is now sorted out while the app is still open: your stage credit arrives as it would after any other run, and after a Long run the coach’s words and your next prescription come with it. After any other kind of run there is nothing from the coach to wait for — a Long run is the only one that wakes it, race or no race. See Prescriptions in the training plan.

A start-up job that hits a problem no longer takes the app with it. Those catch-up jobs run every time you open the app, and one of them failing used to shut the app down before you saw a screen. Each of them now fails on its own, quietly, and its work waits for the next time you open the app. What is caught is a job going wrong. What is not, deliberately, is the app or the phone being in real trouble underneath it — out of room to work in, or something broken in the app itself: the next start would meet the same wall, and an app quietly carrying on past one of those is a worse thing to hand you than a stop. If a run left unfinished is not filled in, opening the app again is still the answer.

If you ever see a run showing 00:00 at the top of your history, with no distance and no heart rate, the app was shut down too quickly to finish tidying up after it. Close the app fully and open it again first. Every time the app starts it finishes off any run that was left unfinished, so if that one recorded anything at all it fills in by itself and is an ordinary run from then on. If it still says 00:00 after a fresh start, there was genuinely nothing in it, and you can delete it — hold it down in the history list, and tap the bin.

The moment you stop

A sheet comes up. Everything on it can also be changed later, from the run's own page, for ever.

How it felt

Rate the effort out of ten and leave a note. These are your words about your run. They never change what your heart rate says about it, and they never graduate you through the plan — but the coach is shown them.

The distance, if it was a treadmill run

Type in the number the console showed. It then counts like any other distance: your pace, your weekly totals, what the coach sees, and the longest-run record. A run nobody typed a distance for shows a dash, not 0.00 km.

A total is not a split. The five fastest-distance records — 1 km, mile, 5 km, 10 km, half marathon — are about the quickest stretch found inside a run, and a total distance cannot say how quick any stretch inside it was. So none of them is ever guessed from what you typed. You can still claim them on a treadmill run by typing the console's own time for each — see Records.

"This was a walk"

One mark on a whole run. The app never guesses it. A walk:

  • builds fitness in full, and pays only a quarter of its score into fatigue — the fatigue that wrecks your form is largely pounding, and walking does not pound;
  • counts towards your goals and fills the weekly bars;
  • takes no record of any kind, completes no prescribed workout, and graduates no stage;
  • leaves every segment and every matched-run group it was in. Unmark it and it is measured back in.

Marking an old run a walk changes the numbers since. Fitness, fatigue and form from that day forward all move. They are a live read of the truth, and the alternative is leaving numbers up that we know are wrong.

What you are told afterwards

One card, and it says who is talking. AI Coach Debrief is words that actually came back from the coach. Plan Update is the app's own arithmetic — a stage granted, a plan finished, a test missed. Older debriefs from before the app started signing them just say Run Debrief, because there is no honest way to work out afterwards which wrote it.

A run's own page

Open any run from History. This is where everything about that one run lives, for as long as you keep it.

This run — the written summary

A few sentences at the very top saying what stood out: your pace and heart rate in context, what the run cost you, any records or segment medals it took, and how it compares with your usual on the same route — 12 seconds a kilometre quicker than usual on this loop.

It is written the first time you open the run, and kept from then on, so opening it again is instant. A run you never open is never sent anywhere. It waits until the app has finished measuring the run against your records, your segments and your other runs, so it can never tell you a run took nothing when it took a medal a second later.

Try again appears if it could not be written — usually no signal. Write it again asks for fresh words when there are already some. Neither one can leave you with a blank card: if the second attempt fails, the words you had stay put.

It needs AI sharing switched on. With it off — or for a run recorded while it was off — nothing is sent and no summary is written. The card still appears, saying so in one line, with no button under it. Switch AI sharing back on and open the run again and it is written then — except for a run you recorded while sharing was off, which is never sent, whatever the switch says today. See Your data.

Where History → any run, at the top

The map

Your route, coloured by the heart-rate zone you were in. Tap for a fullscreen version.

The chart, and the dot

Pace, heart rate and elevation over the run. Drag your finger across it and a dot moves along the route on the map above, so you can see exactly where the hill was.

The numbers

Duration and moving time. Duration is the run's clock, stopped for any pause. Moving time takes off the stretches where you were standing still whether or not the app paused — so if auto-pause is off, or you stopped too briefly for it to catch, the two differ and moving time is the smaller. Then distance, average pace, elevation gain, average and maximum heart rate, effort score, walk breaks, and time in each of the five zones.

Splits

A row per kilometre with its pace, its average heart rate and its climb, and a bar so the slow ones show up at a glance. The last part-kilometre gets its own row.

Medals

Any records this run took. Nothing at all if it took none.

Segments crossed

Every stretch you have marked that this run went over, with your time and where it placed. See Racing yourself.

Matched runs

Which run of this route it was — your 4th run on this route — a chart of your quickest pace on each day you ran it, and a tap through to the list of the others. See Racing yourself.

Keeping this run as a route

Under the map, Save as route keeps the ground this run went over as a course in your library, to run again. Beside it is the way to cut a segment out of the run — a stretch inside it — where this is the whole round. See Routes.

Sharing the run as a file

Garmin (.fit) is the one to use for Garmin Connect and Strava. It states everything rather than leaving it to be worked out — its own distance, duration and moving time, its own splits as laps, and a moment for every second that recorded anything. A treadmill run with no GPS at all is still a whole file, with its heart-rate trace intact.

GPX is the portable option for everything that isn't Garmin: track, times and heart rate per point. A run with no GPS track cannot be one, so it is not offered.

Garmin's phone app cannot take an activity in. Opening a file with it offers to make the file a course instead. Upload the .fit on Garmin Connect's website.

Where the share icon, top right

Deleting a run

Gone, with its heart-rate trace, its GPS and its segment times. Anything that was standing on it is put right: a medal it held is passed back to whatever was behind it, and a coach's prescription that was reasoned from it is withdrawn.

Progress

The screen that answers "am I actually getting fitter, and should I run hard today".

Effort Score — what one run cost you

Every second of a run weighted by the zone it was spent in, added up. Not taken off your average heart rate, which is the whole point: a run/walk session scores its running as running instead of being averaged into mush.

A run that recorded no heart rate has no score rather than a zero — an unmeasured run is not a free one.

Idling is not training. Seconds spent below Zone 1's bottom edge weigh nothing here, so a very easy warm-up can add Zone 1 time to the chart without moving the score. The chart and the score part company on purpose at that one point: the chart's Zone 1 sweeps up everything underneath it so no second vanishes from the picture, while the score refuses to pay for a heart rate that was barely working.

Fitness, Fatigue and Form

  • Fitness — your effort scores averaged over 42 days. Slow to move. What you have built.
  • Fatigue — the same over 7 days. Quick to move. What you are carrying.
  • Form — yesterday's fitness less yesterday's fatigue. How fresh you are this morning, before today's run has cost anything. Above +10 is fresh; below −10 is fatigued.

Weekly volume

A bar per week, and you can flip the whole chart between distance, time and effort.

"Partly measured". A week holding a run that wore no strap says so. Its total is a floor, never a ceiling — otherwise a week you ran hard without the strap would read as a light week.

Goals

A target you set yourself: a distance, a time, or a number of runs, per week, month or year. It recurs with no end date, and it is measured from your runs each time you look — so editing a goal re-measures the period you are in rather than starting a new one.

A goal is yours, not the plan's. Hitting one graduates no stage, and being behind on one can never buy you a harder session from the coach.

The Max HR card

Until you have ever set a maximum, this card asks you to confirm one, so your zones are not left sitting on a placeholder. It offers the highest heart rate the app has recorded from you; if it has never recorded one, it asks your age and offers a rough figure from that. Saying yes works your past runs out again to match.

It asks once, then never again. Set a maximum — here or in Prefs — or put the card away, and it is gone for good. It does not come back later because a run went higher than your setting: a peak above your maximum is yours to act on, by typing the new number into Prefs yourself.

The Records section

All seven records on one card: your best at each, and the day you set it. A record nobody has run yet shows an empty dash and Not run yet rather than being left off — so the card always tells you what there is to go at.

Tap a record and you get its own page. The all-time best is at the top. Under it, Best by day — a chart of your best on each day you contested that record, so you can see whether you are getting better at that record rather than just fitter in general. Then your top ten at it, each row naming the day and opening the run that set it.

The chart needs two days. Contest a record on only one day and there is no chart, because one day is a dot, not a line. It appears on its own once a second day exists — the same rule a segment's trend keeps.

The standings go ten deep even though the medals only go three deep, and they hold every run's effort rather than only the good ones — that is what the chart is drawn from. A line through your ten best days would not be a trend.

Nothing needs mending. Delete a run and its efforts go with it, and whoever was eleventh is tenth the next time you look. Mark a run a walk and it stops contesting everything at once, medals included. Correct a stated distance and the run is measured again from scratch.

"Still measuring your runs". Straight after the update the app works back through your whole history to fill the deeper standings. While that is happening the section says what it is doing rather than showing a top ten drawn from half your runs — a partial table would hand out places you never took. It clears itself when the work is done, and it takes a moment, not an evening. If it is interrupted it picks the job back up the next time you open the app.

Where Progress → Records

What the seven records mean

Gold, silver and bronze at seven records: fastest 1 km, mile, 5 km, 10 km and half marathon, plus longest run and longest time.

The five distances are contested as a best effort — the quickest continuous stretch anywhere inside a run, walk breaks included — not as "a run that happened to be 5 km".

Longest time is contested by every finished run that is not a walk, treadmill included, on its clock alone. It needs no distance at all, so a treadmill run you never typed a number into is still in those standings.

Longest run needs a distance somebody stands behind: measured off a route, or stated off a console. A treadmill run with a stated distance contests it, because a stated distance is a real distance.

A treadmill run can contest the five fastest too, but only because you say so. The distance you type in is a total, and a total cannot say how quick the best kilometre inside it was — so nothing is ever guessed from it. Instead the run's own page lets you type the time the console showed for a given distance, and a time entered that way is ranked against your measured efforts exactly like one of them.

Which distances you are offered. Leave the total blank and you are offered all five, because the two statements are independent and only a distance that is actually there can be too short. Type a total and you are offered the record distances that fit inside it — a run stated at 600 metres is offered nothing, rather than five chips it would have to refuse. Correcting a total downwards withdraws any claim it has just made impossible.

One statement per distance, per run. Typing a time again corrects the one that is there rather than adding a second claim.

Where the run's page → Best efforts

The training plan, and the AI coach

Two different things that share a screen. The plan is arithmetic. The coach is a language model, and it is fenced in hard.

Stages

The plan is an ordered list of stages and you are in exactly one at a time. Later ones show a padlock; the one you are in never does.

Every run writes down the stage it was run under the moment you press START. That is what stops one stage's work graduating the next.

What graduates you

A stage asks for something before it lets go of you.

Where the ask is written in numbers — "a 5K under 30 minutes" — the app measures it and decides it, and the coach is fenced out entirely. It works with AI sharing switched off, and any run can answer it: an open run counts, because a parkrun is the truest 5K there is and a time is a time wherever it turned up.

On a treadmill, type the time, not just the distance. A distance on the finish sheet is a total, and the app will not divide a total by a duration to invent a 5K time. So a treadmill run answers a timed requirement only once you have entered the console's own time for that distance under Best efforts on the run's page. Do that and it graduates you exactly like a measured one.

Where the ask holds a judgement — "four weeks of consistent Zone 2 training" — the coach decides, but not on its own: the app counts the four weeks for it and it judges the consistent. See below.

It is asked late, on purpose. Never at STOP, but once you can no longer change what the run was — so a run you are about to mark a walk cannot graduate anything. Granted forwards only, and never taken back.

The coach is shown all of your training in this stage

A stage that asks for weeks of training needs to be judged on weeks of training. The coach only ever sees your last three sessions, and with a long run, an easy run and a walk in a week those three reach back about a week — so it could never confirm four, and it kept telling you the block was only just beginning.

So the app counts it instead. Every run you have done in the stage is counted, and the coach is handed that total as a fact, along with the weeks broken out one by one. A week you did not run in is shown as a zero rather than left out, because a gap is exactly what consistent is asking about.

What gets counted is a plan run you did under this stage, that ran longer than two minutes, that you did not mark a walk and did not keep from the coach — the same runs the app would let a stage be graduated on, and no others. A run of two minutes or less is treated as a false start rather than a session: it is left out of this count, and out of the three recent runs the coach is shown.

The week-by-week list stops at twelve weeks. Stay in one stage longer than that and the coach is shown the most recent twelve weeks one by one, with the earlier ones left off — and it is told they are left off, so it cannot read the short list as a short stage. The two numbers that matter are always the whole stage however long it has run: how many runs you have done in it, and how long you have been training in it. What the older weeks alone could show — where the gaps fell that far back — is the one thing not carried over.

A week means seven days gone by. Counted from the first run the count accepted — not from the day the stage began, and not from the Monday of that week. An open run, a walk, a run you kept from the coach or a run of two minutes or less does not start the clock, so a stage can be a fortnight old with its first week only just under way. Start on a Sunday and four Mondays go past in a fortnight; four weeks of training arrive on day 28 and not a day sooner. The coach is given the number of whole weeks, and it is told not to count the weeks in the list instead.

The count says how much, never how hard. It is a list of dates and nothing else — no heart rate, no distance, no times. Where a stage asks for both, like “four weeks of consistent Zone 2 training”, the count answers the four weeks and the recent runs answer the Zone 2, and it takes both.

Tests

The one workout that exists to answer a stage's requirement: a 5K flat out, no warm-up or cool-down wrapped round it. The app says one is due three weeks after the last, and holds off while your form is low.

It is a prompt, never a gate — the test stays pickable whether the app has asked for it or not.

An old run can clear the bar

If a run you already did clears the stage's time, the app tells you so and names the run it is talking about, rather than promoting you in silence for something you did weeks ago.

Prescriptions

After you finish a Long run — and only then — the coach writes the intervals and target zone for your next Long run, and only that. It prescribes work; it does not configure the app.

Nothing else wakes it. An Easy run, a Quality run, a test, an open run or a run that skipped the plan all finish without a new prescription being written, so there is no update to wait for after one.

It is told your effort ratings, your notes, the weather you ran in, and where you stand against your goals — and it holds off asking for more when you are carrying too much fatigue.

A prescription stands on the runs it was shown. Delete one of them and the prescription is withdrawn back to the previous one.

Finishing the plan

Finishing the last stage records a plan completion — the day and the time you ran — rather than leaving you parked on the last stage forever printing a target you have already beaten. It survives a restore.

AI sharing

Sharing is on by default and switched off in Prefs. Each run records whether it was on at the moment you pressed START, and that stored answer — not today's setting — decides whether that run can ever be sent. There is no separate per-run switch.

Where Home → Prefs

Racing yourself

Two ways to compare a run against your own past. One you set up; one happens on its own.

Segments — a stretch you named

Mark out a stretch of ground on a past run's own track and name it: "Cemetery Hill", the last kilometre home. You drag the two ends along the line of a run you actually ran — it is never drawn freehand, and it refuses to be dragged across a pause or a lost signal, because the straight line over one of those is ground nothing witnessed.

The shape is copied onto the segment, so deleting the run you cut it from keeps the place.

Where a run's page → New segment from this run · Home → Open Segments

What counts as having run it

A gate at each end and a corridor between them. You have to pass within about thirty metres of the start, stay on the line, and come out within about thirty metres of the end. A GPS blip flung sideways for a second is forgiven; a shortcut that skips part of the stretch is not.

The time is the wall clock between the two gate crossings, worked out between the fixes either side of each gate rather than at the nearest one — on a short stretch that is the difference between a PR and not.

It arrives with your history already on it

Creating a segment measures your whole history against it there and then, so it opens carrying your efforts and your best time instead of an empty page waiting for your next run. Every run that finishes afterwards is put to every segment by the very same pass, which is what stops the two ever disagreeing.

One segment's page gives you a trend of your quickest time on each day you ran it, and the ten fastest efforts with medals.

Matched runs — a route you never named

Nothing to set up. When a run finishes the app takes the shape of it: where you started, where you finished, three points evenly spaced along the way, and how far you went. Two runs whose shapes agree are the same route. Anything under half a kilometre is left out — see below.

Agreeing means: starts and ends within about a hundred metres of each other, distances within a twentieth of each other, and the ground between covered in the same order.

  • An out-and-back done the other way round still counts — it retraces the same line past the same places, so it really is the same route.
  • A loop done backwards does not — the middle of it is mirrored, and that is a different route.
  • A longer run that merely starts and finishes in the same places does not — the distances disagree.

What the card shows

Which run of that route this one was, how many there are, a chart of your quickest pace on each day you ran it, and a tap through to the list. If every run of a route happened on one day there is no chart, just the count — one day is not a trend.

Nothing is stored about the group

The group is worked out fresh each time you open a page. That is why deleting a run needs no repair at all: it simply stops being in every group it was in, and the other runs' counts correct themselves.

A walk never joins a group, and neither does a treadmill run, which has no ground to have covered. Mark a run a walk and it leaves; unmark it and it comes back.

Nor does anything under half a kilometre. Too little ground to have a shape worth comparing — five points on 300 metres would match half the neighbourhood — so short repetitions over identical ground stay ungrouped by design.

Routes

A library of courses you keep. Separate from your history — the two never touch.

The library

Every course you keep, with its distance and its elevation gain. A course whose heights were never recorded says so rather than showing 0 m.

Beside each one, a small drawing of its shape — the park loop, the canal out-and-back — so you can pick the one you want without reading the names. It is the same little drawing your runs have in History, in the same amber line, so the two lists read as one thing.

It is an outline and nothing else: one amber line, no map underneath, no streets, and none of the heart-rate colours a run’s own map is drawn in. At that size streets are a smudge, and the drawing has one job, which is letting you recognise a course at a glance. The course’s real map is on the run page when you run it.

The square is always there, drawn or not. A course too small to have a shape — one whose whole course fits inside a square sixty metres across, so a lap of a small yard however many times you go round it — keeps an empty square rather than showing a dot pretending to be a route. The square is held open either way so the names stay in a straight line down the list, and so nothing shuffles under your finger when a drawing appears a moment after the list does.

Where Home → Open Routes

What this update did to your library

The app changed how it tidies a course, and that change only reached courses arriving from then on. Everything already in your library was still stored the old way, so the app could be handed one of your own route files and fail to recognise its own library. Updating the app redraws every route once, the new way, so from here on a course is stored one way and recognised one way.

Three things you may notice, each of them once:

  • A distance can come out slightly shorter. Tidying a course keeps its shape and drops points along the way, and the distance is measured along the line that is kept, so it cuts a little off the corners. On one real course of 4.20 km that came to about 47 m. Courses that came in from a file are where this shows; a course saved off a run was already tidied, and mostly does not move at all.
  • A course you happened to keep twice becomes one. Saved off a run and imported from that same run’s file, it was two rows; it is now one. The one you have had longest stays, under the name you have been seeing. A run that was following the copy that went is pointed at the one that stayed. If the copy that went was the course you had picked for your next run, the picker on the home screen goes back to Choose a route — pick the surviving one and set off as normal. Your pick is only ever held until you press START, so this is the one thing here you may have to do again.
  • The climb is not worked out again. A route keeps a line and a number, not the heights the number was worked out from, and the file it came from is not kept — so no climb here is ever re-measured or replaced. The one way a climb moves is the merge above: where the copy that stays had no climb recorded and a copy it absorbed had one, it takes that one, because “no climb recorded” is the app having nothing to say about the course rather than a claim that it is flat. A climb that is already there is never overwritten. Import that course’s file again and the climb is measured afresh from the file.

Nothing else moves. Names, the date a route was kept and how it got in are untouched, and no run is changed. A route whose line the app cannot read at all is left exactly as it is, rather than guessed at.

Where Nothing to do — it happens by itself, the first time you open the updated app

Getting a route in

Three ways, all landing in the same library. From the picker on the Routes screen; by choosing this app from Open with on a .gpx file — a download, an email attachment, a Strava or Komoot export; or off a run you have already been for, with Save as route on the run's page.

A route from a file takes its name from inside the GPX, or from the filename if the file doesn't name itself. A file that isn't a GPX, is damaged, or holds no route is refused with a message saying why, and nothing is saved. A route off a run is named after that run.

Importing the same course again

Import a .gpx file drawn along a course you already keep and you get no second copy. The line is what the app knows a course by, so it recognises it and tells you which of three things happened:

  • Nothing was added. The file measures the course exactly as your library already has it. “That route is already in your routes, as ‘Willingdon Circle’. Nothing was added.”
  • Its numbers were refreshed. The file measures the course differently, so the distance and climb you keep now come from this file. “‘Willingdon Circle’ is already in your routes. Its distance and climb now come from this file.”
  • Its distance was refreshed, and its climb kept. Same as above, but the file carries no heights and the course already had a climb. “… Its distance now comes from this file. The file carries no heights, so its climb is unchanged.”

A file that says nothing about climb cannot take your climb away. Plenty of GPX files carry no heights at all — a course traced on a map, an export that dropped them. Re-importing one of those over a course that does have a climb used to wipe the climb out and leave “no elevation recorded”, with no way back but finding the first file again. It now keeps the climb, and the message says so plainly rather than staying quiet about it.

Your name for it is never overwritten. Rename a course and re-import its file, and the name you gave it stands. Only the distance and the climb are ever refreshed.

An older route is recognised now too. Routes kept before this update were stored the old way, so handing back one of their files could give you a second copy. Updating the app redrew them all, and that no longer happens — see What this update did to your library above. One rare case is left: a course you saved off a run before 30 August 2026, whose own .gpx you then hand back, can still arrive as a second copy, because a point the old tidying dropped cannot be put back. If it happens, delete whichever copy you do not want.

Where Open with on a .gpx file → Running App · Home → Open Routes → the picker

Keeping a run as a route

On a finished run's page, under the map, Save as route keeps the ground that run went over as a course you can keep and rename. The run itself is not touched and nothing links the two afterwards — delete either and the other is unharmed.

You are told which of three things happened, in words:

  • Kept. It names what the course is called, which is what the run is called — Run 22 Aug 2026, 06:21, the same words the run is shared under.
  • Already kept. Saving the same run twice makes one course, not two, and the message names the row you already have. If you have since renamed it, that is the name you are told.
  • Nothing to keep. A run that never got a fix, or one that only ever wandered on the spot.

The distance shown is not the run's distance. A route's distance is how far the course goes for whoever follows it — one clean line, straightened out and joined across any pause. Your run counted every metre it actually covered. The two are close, and they are not meant to match.

Only where there is a track. A treadmill run has no ground to keep, so it gets no button rather than a button that refuses. The same goes for history from before the app recorded tracks.

Saving a run and importing that same run’s GPX gives you one route, not two. Both doors now tidy the course to its shape the same way, so the app sees one course and points you at the route you already have, rather than adding a second. It will usually say the route’s numbers have been refreshed from the file — a .gpx states heights a little more roughly than the run itself held them, so the climb it works out is a shade different. That is one route, brought up to date, not two. This holds for a run you save from here on; a run you saved as a course before 30 August 2026 is the rare exception above. It is also only true of files this app made: a course you exported to Strava or Komoot and brought back is tidied by their rules, which draw a different line, so that arrives as a route of its own.

Where History → any run with a map → under the map

Running one

Pick a course on the home screen before you start — see Following a route. During the run the map card draws the course in blue, underneath the amber line of where you have actually been. Amber sitting on blue means you are on the course; blue showing on its own is course you have not run yet.

In the bottom-right corner of the map, how much of the course is left: 5.54 km to go while there are kilometres in it, then plain metres — 840 m to go — once you are inside the last one. Before you reach the course it reads as the whole course, because you have not run any of it yet.

The number is measured from where you actually are to the nearest point on the course, and on to the end. On an out-and-back, where the way out and the way back lie over the same ground, it knows which of the two you are on: it reads from where you were a moment ago rather than from the whole line. Run the course backwards and it counts down to the end you set off towards.

It works with the map downloaded for offline use, because the course comes out of your own library rather than off the internet.

It keeps counting when you are nowhere near the course. Wander off and the number carries on measuring to the end from the nearest bit of course to you. Being told you have strayed is a separate thing, and it is spoken rather than shown — see Off course below.

The distance left is never spoken. It is on the screen only — no cue, no milestone. The only things the app says out loud about a route are the two in Off course below, and neither of them mentions how far is left.

Delete a route mid-run and the run is fine. The blue line stops being drawn and the corner number goes with it. The run carries on recording where you actually go, which was never the route’s business.

The full-screen map is not reachable at the moment. Tapping the map card is meant to open it, and does nothing — a fault that has been there since the map was built, not something this brought in. The course is drawn there too, so it will simply appear once tapping works again. Until then the card is the whole of it, and the camera stays close to you rather than pulling back to hold the whole course.

Off course

On a run following a route, the app says two things and no more. Go more than 50 m from the course and stay out there for 10 seconds, and it says “Off course.” Come back within 30 m of it, and it says “Back on course.”

Nothing at all is said in between. A detour you meant to take — round a closed path, out to a shop, a lap of the park — costs you exactly two sentences, whether you are away for one minute or twenty. Go off again later and you are told the same way again.

It never tells you which way to turn. The app knows where the course is and where you are. It has no idea what is between the two — which streets exist, which way they run, what is a wall. So it tells you the one thing it actually knows, and leaves the navigating to you.

The 50 m and the 30 m are deliberately different numbers. 50 m is a street away, not a wide pavement: a course drawn down the middle of a road, running on the far side of a dual carriageway, and a GPX traced off somebody else’s run all put honest running tens of metres off the line. Coming back within 30 m is the same closeness that counts as reaching the course in the first place. Two numbers rather than one so that hovering either side of a single line does not have the app telling you off and back and off again for the length of a street.

  • Silent until you have reached the course. A run starts at your front door, and the walk or jog to the start of the route is not a wrong turn. Nothing is said, however far off the line you are, until you have been within 30 m of the course at least once.
  • A poor fix is not counted. A reading counts only if the GPS is sure of you to 30 m or better. One worse than that — or one that cannot say how sure it is at all — is not measured against the course. A fix that could be 100 m out on its own admission is no evidence of a wrong turn. Any 10 seconds part-way through starts again from the next good fix.
  • Standing still is not counted either. While the app has auto-paused you for standing still, the same applies. You are not running off course; you are not running.
  • A pause starts the 10 seconds again. Pause out there by hand and the wait begins again when you resume, so you are never told off for something you did five minutes before you stopped. What you have already been told still stands: pause while off course, resume, and you are still off course, and you will still be told when you get back.
  • It goes to the front of the queue. Ahead of a split, ahead of an interval change, ahead of the coach — going the wrong way gets worse the longer it takes to say. It still never cuts off a sentence already being spoken; nothing in the app does.

Only on a run following a route. An ordinary run has no course to be off, so there is nothing to say and nothing is said.

Delete the route mid-run and it goes quiet. The blue line stops being drawn and no further cue is made, the same promise the map makes: deleting a route costs the run nothing. One that had just been made and was still waiting its turn is taken back with the route — you will not be told about a course the app no longer has. The only sentence that still finishes is one already being spoken, because nothing in the app ever cuts a sentence off.

Your data

Everything lives on the phone. This is the part that means it isn't only on the phone.

What is stored where

  • Your runs and their heart-rate traces and GPS — a database on the phone.
  • Your settings, your plan and your prescriptions — separate phone storage.
  • The Run Journal — a plain text file.

The one rule that matters. Your run history lives in one place only. Never uninstall the app to swap versions — install over the top instead.

The archive

One ZIP holding a GPX for each finished run that has a usable track, a file of everything GPX cannot carry, and a snapshot of the whole database. Written to the folder you picked, automatically each month, keeping the last three — plus a Back up now button and the time of the last one.

Every run is in there either way. A treadmill run, a run still being recorded, or one whose GPS was all too vague to trust rides in the database snapshot rather than as a GPX — because a GPX of a run that went nowhere would be an empty file with a name on it.

Where Home → Prefs → Backup

Restore

Pick an archive and the app opens the database inside it before swapping anything, so a file it cannot read is refused rather than leaving you with nothing.

History restored from an older file is re-banded on the heart rates it was actually recorded under — the maximum and the resting one in force then, not today's.

The Run Journal

The app's own plain-text record of what decided whether a run was recording — the run starting, pausing and stopping, the recording being promoted, refused or handed back, the strap arriving or being given up on.

It exists because Android's own log holds about two hours, and a run plus the walk home is longer, so the minute that would name a cause had already rolled off before anyone could look. It is capped, it rolls, it rides in the archive, and nothing in the app reads it back to make a decision.

The app's words

Words that mean something exact here, and where they will trip you up if you assume.

Moving time
How long you were actually going somewhere. Duration is the run's own clock, and it already stops for a pause. Moving time then takes off the sustained spells where the GPS shows you covering no ground at all — so it can be shorter than duration on a run with no pause in it, and it is never longer.
Pause
You stopped, by hand or because the app saw you standing still. Carries no distance and no time.
Outage
The GPS signal dropped while you kept running. Different from a pause: the app carries the straight line across it, because you really did cover that ground.
Walk
A mark you put on a whole run. Full fitness, a quarter of the fatigue, no records, no graduation. Never guessed by the app.
Stated distance
The number you typed off a treadmill console. A real distance for pace, volume, the coach and the longest-run record. It never wins one of the five fastest-distance records, because a total cannot say how quick a stretch inside it was — those are claimed by typing the console's time for the stretch itself.
Stated best effort
A time you typed off a treadmill console for one record distance inside a run. Ranked against your measured efforts like one of them. One per distance, per run.
Best effort
The quickest continuous stretch of a given distance found anywhere inside a run — not a run that happened to be that long.
Standings
The top ten at one record, on that record's own page. Deeper than the medals, which stop at three. Built from every run's effort at that record, not only the ten best — that is what the trend chart is drawn from. "Effort" means whatever that record measures: a time over a set distance for the five fastest, a distance for longest run, a clock for longest time.
Zone
One of five fixed slices of your reserve — the gap between your maximum heart rate and your resting one. With no resting rate typed the reserve starts at zero, and the zones are plain percentages of your maximum.
Effort Score
What one run cost you, as one number. Every second weighted by its zone — except seconds below Zone 1's bottom edge, which weigh nothing even though the chart still shows them as Zone 1. Idling is not training.
Form
Fitness minus fatigue, as of yesterday. How fresh you are before today's run.
Stage
One block of the training plan. You are in exactly one at a time, and every run records which.
Stage training record
The app's own count of every run you have done in the stage you are in, handed to the AI coach as a fact, with the most recent twelve weeks broken out one by one and any earlier ones named as left off. It counts plan runs under that stage that ran longer than two minutes, that you did not mark a walk and did not keep from the coach — the same runs a stage can be graduated on. A week you did not run in is a zero, not a gap. It says how much you trained and never how hard, and a week in it means seven days gone by from the first run it counted, so four weeks arrive on day 28.
Prescription
The intervals and target zone the AI coach wrote for your next Long run. Written only after you finish a Long run, and it stands on the runs it was shown.
Route
A course in your library: a line to follow and what following it costs. A plan, not a recording — it carries no date, no heart rate and no times, and it may be run any number of times or never. It gets in from a GPX file or off a run you have already been for. The app knows one by its line, so a second file drawn along the same course refreshes the one you have rather than making a copy. Deleting one touches no run.
Segment
A stretch of ground you marked and named. Every run that crosses it is timed. Part of a run, where a route is a whole course.
Run summary
The few sentences the AI writes about one run, at the top of that run's own page. Written the first time you open the run and kept. It describes that run and nothing else — it never prescribes a workout; that is the coach's job.
Routed run
A run you started on a route. An ordinary run with a course written on it and a second, blue line on its live map. Which route is fixed the moment you press START.
Off course
More than 50 m from the route you are following, for 10 seconds or more — and only once you have actually reached that course. Something the app says about you, during one run; it is never a thing that is true of a route. You stop being off course when you come back within 30 m of the line.
Matched runs
Runs the app worked out are the same course, by shape alone. Nothing named, nothing saved, and no route in your library is involved. Runs under half a kilometre have no shape and join nothing.
